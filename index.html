t-SNE & UMAP: Exploring Data in Lower Dimensions

t-SNE gave SwiftParcel beautiful maps — but it's slow and can't place new points. Here's how UMAP builds a faster, reusable map from a neighbourhood graph, what its two dials actually control, and the specific ways both methods can quietly mislead you.

The Dashboard That Takes All Night

In Class 2, Meera at SwiftParcel gave the operations team a t-SNE map of handwritten digit scans, and they loved it. Now the returns department wants the same thing for something bigger: photos of returned items (clothes, shoes, bags) from all 40 hubs.

They want three things:

  1. A map where similar items sit together, to see which categories get mixed up in returns (shirts vs. T-shirts, sandals vs. sneakers).
  2. A map that refreshes every day, on millions of photos. Meera's first t-SNE run on a small sample already takes minutes.
  3. The ability to drop in today's new photos onto the existing map, without redrawing everything and shuffling every island.

Meera remembers her own warnings from last class: t-SNE is slow, it has no transform for new data, and gaps between islands mean nothing. She needs a newer tool. Today: UMAP — much faster, can place new points on an existing map, and often keeps the overall layout more sensible. We'll also learn to be sceptical of it. It has its own ways of misleading you.

Section 1

1t-SNE in One Page

From Class 2, t-SNE works in three steps:

  1. High-D similarities: for each point i, a Gaussian (width set by perplexity) gives the probability pij that j is a neighbour.
  2. Low-D similarities: a heavy-tailed Student t-distribution (1 degree of freedom) gives qij in the map — the heavy tails relieve the crowding problem.
  3. Match them: minimise the KL divergence between P and Q.

What are the "learning parameters" of t-SNE?

Not weights of a model. The parameters being optimised are the 2D coordinates yi of every point. Gradient descent literally slides each point around the map. This is why t-SNE has no model to reuse on new data: there is just a set of positions, and no function from input to output.

Why does KL divergence punish some errors more than others?

KL is asymmetric. If two points are neighbours in high-D (pij large) but far apart in the map (qij small), the term p·log(p/q) becomes large — tearing neighbours apart is expensive. If two points are far apart in high-D (pij near zero) but close in the map, the penalty is small — putting strangers together is cheap.

So t-SNE protects local neighbourhoods and is relaxed about what happens between groups. That is why gaps and cluster sizes are not trustworthy.

Perplexity is the effective number of neighbours considered per point. Small values give a local view (fragmented); large values give a more global view (but slower and blurrier).

Quick check

Meera doubles the perplexity from 30 to 60. Which statement about the effect is most accurate?

Correct. Perplexity is roughly the effective number of neighbours. A larger value means each point looks at more neighbours, so the view shifts from local toward global, and the extra neighbour computations make the run slower. It doesn't change the number of points, and KL divergence is still the objective.

2A Better Test Bed: Returned Items (Fashion‑MNIST)

Digits are a good start, but they have little hierarchy. Fashion-MNIST has 10 item types in 28×28 images (784 features), and the categories have a real story:

  • T-shirt, shirt, pullover, coat and dress are all tops or upper-body clothing.
  • Sandal, sneaker and ankle boot are all footwear.
  • Trousers and bags are different from everything else.

A good map should put footwear together, clothing together, and bags and trousers apart — perfect for checking whether a method keeps both local and global structure. 8,000 random images are reduced to 50 dimensions with PCA first (the standard recipe from Class 2).

Baseline t-SNE run

  • t-SNE separates the categories into clear islands — the local structure we wanted.
  • Look at where the islands sit. Do footwear islands sit near each other? Is bag far from clothing? This is only partly reliable, because gaps in a t-SNE map are not true distances.
  • The run took a noticeable amount of time on just 8,000 points. Meera needs millions.

Section 3

3UMAP Intuition: Build a Graph, Then Draw It

UMAP stands for Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. The name is heavy, but the idea is simple:

Describe the data as a weighted neighbourhood graph, then find a 2D layout whose graph looks the same.

Why is UMAP faster?

  1. It works with a sparse kNN graph (each point has only about k neighbours that matter), rather than similarities between all pairs of points.
  2. It does not need to normalise over all pairs.
  3. The optimisation samples edges and a few random "push apart" points, instead of touching every pair at every step.

The formal theory (fuzzy simplicial sets, topology) is beyond our scope. What matters is: neighbourhood graph in, matching layout out.

Quick check

Which pair of ideas best explains why UMAP typically runs faster than t-SNE on large datasets?

Correct. UMAP's graph is sparse (only about k edges per point), and its optimisation samples edges and a few random repulsive pairs rather than considering every pair at every step. It doesn't discard data, ignore neighbours, or require GPUs.

Interpretation: look for three things

  1. Local structure. Are individual categories grouped into islands? Usually both methods manage this — UMAP's islands often look tighter, with more empty space between them.
  2. Global layout. Do related categories (footwear: sandal, sneaker, ankle boot; tops: T-shirt, shirt, pullover, coat) sit close together, with bags and trousers off by themselves? UMAP often shows a more interpretable arrangement, though t-SNE can show it too with PCA initialisation — don't expect a huge gap.
  3. Hard cases. Shirt, T-shirt, pullover and coat blend together in both maps. That's not a failure — those items really do look alike, and the map is telling you something true about the data.
A note on claims you'll read online

Published demos report UMAP running many times faster than t-SNE. The exact ratio depends on hardware, library versions and settings — which is why it was measured directly above. Likewise, "UMAP preserves global structure and t-SNE does not" is too simple. A large share of the difference comes from initialisation, so judge it on your own data.

Quick check

Meera's map shows each category as a few tight, fragmented specks and no overall layout. She wants smoother, more global structure without changing how densely points are packed. Which change should she try?

Correct. Fragmentation with weak global layout is a sign that each point sees too few neighbours. Raising n_neighbors broadens the neighbourhoods and restores overall structure. min_dist only changes how tightly points are packed, not what structure is found, and a smaller n_neighbors would make fragmentation worse.

Section 6

6The Feature Meera Needed: Placing New Photos on the Old Map

Requirement 3: "drop in today's photos without redrawing the map." t-SNE cannot do this — it has only coordinates. UMAP can, because it learns how to connect a new point to its neighbours among the existing points and places it accordingly. transform() does this approximately.

Quick check

SwiftParcel wants (1) a daily-refreshed map, (2) new photos added without moving existing points, and (3) a quick overview with a small number of settings to tune. Which choice fits best, and what is the main caveat?

Correct. UMAP is faster and supports transform for new points. The caveat: placing new points is an approximation, and hyperparameters (n_neighbors, min_dist) still matter. PCA also supports transform and is far faster, but it's linear and won't separate the islands as well. t-SNE has no transform, so it fails requirement 2.
Section 7

7Where UMAP Misleads

UMAP pictures are persuasive, which is exactly why they need care.

Demo 1: pure random noise

There is no structure at all in the data below — just independent random numbers. What does UMAP draw?

The data has no groups, yet at small n_neighbors you'll typically see clumps and strands, as UMAP faithfully draws the accidental neighbourhoods that random points have.

A pretty island on a UMAP map is a hypothesis, not a finding. Confirm it in the original data (or with a different seed or setting) before acting on it.

Demo 2: a dense cluster inside a sparse one

UMAP preserves neighbourhood connectivity but not density, which can hide one kind of structure: a tight, dense group sitting inside a much more spread-out group.

Compare how clearly each method separates the dense core from the surrounding cloud. The documented weakness: UMAP can fail to keep the two apart, because both groups look like well-connected neighbourhoods and UMAP doesn't preserve density. t-SNE's per-point scaling often separates them more cleanly. Your own run may differ in detail. Variants of UMAP that try to preserve density exist (for example, densMAP).

Quick check

A UMAP map of delivery scans shows one large island and one small island. The analyst reports: "The large island is a much bigger group of scans, and its distance from the small island shows the two types are very different." What is the correct response?

Correct. Island size is influenced by density and settings, and distances between islands are not preserved as true distances. What can be trusted is mainly that points in the same island are neighbours. Claims about group size or dissimilarity should be verified with counts and distances in the original (or PCA) space — min_dist has no bearing on that.

A practical workflow

  1. Scale the features (when units differ) and run PCA to about 50 dimensions.
  2. Draw a UMAP map for speed, and a t-SNE map as a cross-check.
  3. Try several settings. Trust only the structure that survives across settings and methods.
  4. Treat every island as a hypothesis, and verify it in the original data.

Wrap-Up: Closing the SwiftParcel Story

Three classes ago, Meera had millions of scans, no labels, and a question she couldn't answer: "What does this data look like?" Today she walks into the returns department with the final answer.

  • Storage and speed (Class 1): PCA shrank each image from 784 numbers to a few dozen, so the pipeline became light enough to run every day.
  • Neighbourhood maps (Class 2): t-SNE turned the overlapping blob of digits into distinct islands, and taught her which parts of a map can be trusted.
  • A map that scales (Class 3): UMAP now refreshes the returned-item map daily, places each morning's new photos onto the existing layout with transform, and keeps the map stable for managers who've learned where "shoes" and "bags" live.

The pipeline, not a single algorithm

Scale → PCA to about 50 dimensions → UMAP for the daily map, with t-SNE as a cross-check on anything surprising. Before anyone acts on an island, Meera confirms it in the original data. The managers get a map they can use, and the team gets a clear rule for when the picture can be trusted.

Key takeaways

  • t-SNE learns 2D coordinates directly by minimising KL divergence. KL is asymmetric, so it protects local neighbourhoods. It's slow and has no transform.
  • UMAP builds a fuzzy k-nearest-neighbour graph, then finds a 2D layout whose graph matches it. It's faster because the graph is sparse and the optimisation samples edges.
  • n_neighbors controls what counts as structure (local vs. global). min_dist controls how tightly points pack in the picture.
  • UMAP supports approximate transform for new data, which matters in production.
  • Both methods can mislead: island sizes and gaps are not meaningful, noise can look like structure, results change with seeds, and UMAP can blur dense-in-sparse structure.
  • "UMAP is always better" and "UMAP preserves global structure and t-SNE does not" are too simple — measure on your own data.
  • Treat every island as a hypothesis, and verify it in the original data before acting.
